\title{How much does wind cost? A paired calm/windy synthetic RGB-D benchmark that separates occlusion, sensor and wind error in simulated 3D foliage reconstruction}

\author[1]{Bibek Gyawali\corref{cor1}}

\begin{abstract}
Neural scene representations such as 3D Gaussian Splatting and neural radiance fields are increasingly used to reconstruct plants for phenotyping, but they assume a static scene, while foliage is never still outdoors or near fans and ventilation. Existing plant datasets cannot tell how much of the reconstruction error is caused by the method and occlusion, by the depth sensor, or by leaf motion, because ground truth for moving leaves is unavailable. We present SynthTree, a fully scripted generator of paired calm/windy RGB-D sequences of potted saplings, modelled on a common hand-held RGB-D capture protocol (Intel RealSense D435i, fan-generated wind). Every tree is rendered at several canopy wind speeds with a bit-identical camera trajectory; leaves are real geometry with unique identities, and a closed-form wind model (hierarchical branch sway and rigid leaf flutter) makes the exported per-leaf rigid transforms exact by construction. Automatic checks on all \NSeqValidated{} sequences confirm sub-pixel consistency of poses, intrinsics, depth and per-leaf motion (re-projection error $\le$\,\ValSelfReprojPx\,px; per-leaf transforms predict the rendered optical flow to \ValLeafFlowPx\,px). We further propose a three-way error decomposition with an explicit interaction term and a geometry-first evaluation protocol for Gaussian reconstructions. In \NRunsTotal{} training runs on four trees (one per leaf type), wind at \WindMax\,m/s reduces the leaf F-score of 3D Gaussian Splatting (Nerfstudio splatfacto) by \SpWindHiF{} and of a depth-supervised NeRF by \NfWindHiF{}, and raises the per-leaf centroid error of splatfacto by \SpWindHiCentroid\,mm, while image-space PSNR changes by only \RelChangePSNR\,\%: in this setting, view-synthesis metrics hide most of the geometric damage. Simulated sensor noise, in contrast, mainly trades precision for coverage. Using the exported per-leaf motion, we further show that, for our static 3DGS baseline, evaluating against the rest pose over-states the wind error by \StWindDropOverPct\,\%, and that a robust trimmed loss helps in all conditions but does not reduce the wind component. A deformation-field Gaussian baseline collapses to a uniform shift; a follow-up at the full 30\,fps shows that this is a failure of the motion model rather than of temporal sampling. Per-part rigid motion removes the collapse and reduces the wind part of the chamfer error by \SoClWindCDRedPct\,\%, but even with ground-truth leaf grouping about three quarters of it remain. Neither four times denser sampling nor a second synchronised camera reduces the wind error; the learned leaf motion stays far smaller than the true motion, which points to motion estimation as the remaining bottleneck. All experiments are synthetic, with sensor and wind models set to plausible, literature-informed defaults and without motion blur or rolling shutter; the findings hold in simulation under these models and remain to be confirmed on real captures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Three-dimensional reconstruction of plants underpins non-destructive phenotyping of leaf area, leaf angle distribution and canopy architecture \citep{paulus2019crops3d}. Recent neural representations, neural radiance fields \citep{mildenhall2020nerf,muller2022instant} and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) \citep{kerbl2023gaussian}, produce photorealistic novel views from ordinary image or RGB-D sequences and are rapidly being adopted for plants. All of them assume a static scene. Leaves, however, flutter at a few hertz in a light breeze and whole branches sway \citep{delangre2008wind}, and capturing a full orbit around a sapling takes tens of seconds.

When a reconstruction of a real plant is poor, the cause is ambiguous: thin, self-occluding foliage is intrinsically hard for the method; consumer depth sensors add noise, holes and flying pixels at leaf boundaries \citep{khoshelham2012kinect,keselman2017realsense}; and leaf motion violates the static-scene assumption. These effects cannot be separated on real data, because the true geometry of a moving canopy is unknown, and existing benchmarks either capture static scenes \citep{jensen2014dtu,knapitsch2017tanks,sturm2012tum} or provide plant point clouds without per-leaf motion \citep{schunck2021pheno4d}.

We address this with a controlled synthetic benchmark, modelled on a common capture protocol (a hand-held orbit around a potted sapling with a consumer RGB-D camera and a fan), in which every factor is controlled and every leaf is tracked exactly. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{SynthTree}, an open, fully scripted pipeline (procedural saplings, exact procedural wind, D435i-like RGB-D rendering, sensor-noise simulation with a calibration procedure verified on synthetic recordings) that produces \emph{paired} calm/windy sequences: same tree, bit-identical camera path, only the wind speed differs.
\item \textbf{Exact per-leaf ground truth under wind}: leaf identity maps, per-leaf rigid transforms, optical flow and canonical correspondences, validated to sub-pixel accuracy on all sequences.
\item \textbf{A three-way error decomposition} into occlusion/method, sensor and wind components with an explicit interaction term, and a geometry-first evaluation protocol for Gaussian reconstructions that is robust to floaters and floor splats.
\item \textbf{A benchmark} showing that, in simulation under default sensor and wind models and for the methods tested, wind dominates the sensor effect, view-synthesis metrics substantially under-report the geometric degradation of foliage, and rest-pose evaluation over-states the wind error of a static reconstruction; time-resolved per-leaf ground truth corrects this and allows dynamic methods to be scored.
\item \textbf{A sampling-versus-modelling analysis} of dynamic Gaussian reconstruction under wind: the collapse of a deformation field persists at 30\,fps, per-part rigid motion removes it but recovers little geometry even with ground-truth leaf groups, and neither denser sampling (without motion blur) nor a second synchronised camera reduces the wind error.
\end{enumerate}
Code and data are released (\cref{sec:availability}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{3D plant reconstruction and phenotyping} Laser scanning, multi-view stereo and RGB-D cameras are established for measuring plant geometry \citep{paulus2019crops3d}; Pheno4D \citep{schunck2021pheno4d} provides spatio-temporal point clouds of growing plants with organ labels. Spatio-temporal analysis of plants has so far addressed growth over hours to days, tracking and matching organs between successive scans \citep{li2013growing}; at that time scale wind-induced motion within one scan is treated as noise, not measured. Wind-induced motion of plants is well characterised biomechanically, from slow branch sway to leaf flutter at several hertz \citep{delangre2008wind}, but its effect on reconstruction accuracy has not been quantified. Existing datasets are acquired in still air and, being real, cannot provide the true geometry of moving leaves.

\paragraph{Neural reconstruction of static and dynamic scenes} NeRF \citep{mildenhall2020nerf} and its accelerated variants \citep{muller2022instant}, and 3DGS \citep{kerbl2023gaussian} and its surface-oriented variant 2DGS \citep{huang20242dgs}, reconstruct static scenes; Nerfstudio \citep{tancik2023nerfstudio} provides the implementations we benchmark. Dynamic extensions model deformation over time \citep{pumarola2021dnerf,luiten2024dynamic,wu20244dgs,yang2024deformable}. Per-part and motion-basis variants assign Gaussians to superpoints with rigid motion \citep{wan2024superpoint}, to sparse control points \citep{huang2024scgs} or to a small set of shared SE(3) motion bases \citep{wang2024shape}, or give each Gaussian a polynomial and Fourier trajectory \citep{lin2024gaussianflow}; monocular dynamic reconstruction is further limited by how far the camera moves relative to the scene motion \citep{gao2022monocular}. Gaussian-splatting SLAM \citep{matsuki2024monogs} tracks RGB-D cameras. Robust variants instead treat inconsistent content as outliers, either with per-image appearance and transient embeddings \citep{martinbrualla2021nerfw} or by down-weighting pixels with large residuals \citep{sabour2023robustnerf}; our trimmed-loss mitigation follows the latter idea. How these methods degrade on fluttering foliage has not been quantified with ground truth.

\paragraph{Synthetic data and benchmarks} Rendering pipelines such as BlenderProc \citep{denninger2023blenderproc2} and ICL-NUIM \citep{handa2014iclnuim} provide exact ground truth for rigid indoor scenes, and procedural plant models have a long history \citep{prusinkiewicz1990abop,weber1995trees,runions2007space}. To our knowledge no benchmark provides paired static/dynamic foliage with per-leaf motion ground truth and a consumer RGB-D sensor model.

\paragraph{Depth-sensor noise} Depth noise of structured-light and active-stereo cameras grows approximately quadratically with distance and concentrates at depth discontinuities \citep{khoshelham2012kinect,nguyen2012kinectnoise,keselman2017realsense}, which is precisely where thin leaves are.

\subsection{Procedural saplings with leaf identities}
Each tree is a skeleton of nodes (position, radius, parent) grouped into branches of increasing order, grown with stochastic wander, phototropic up-turn and phyllotactic or whorled branching \citep{weber1995trees}. Wood is meshed as parallel-transported tubes. Leaf sites are spaced along branch segments; a leaf is attached at the bark surface of a segment and inherits that segment's frame. Leaf blades are real triangulated surfaces with species-dependent outlines (ovate, elliptic, obovate, lanceolate), cupping and droop; compound leaves consist of a rachis and leaflets, and needles are closed triangular prisms. Every leaf has an integer identity, an attachment point, a parent branch, a rest frame, an area and a centroid, stored with the canonical (rest) mesh (\cref{tab:trees}). Heights range from \HeightMin{} to \HeightMax\,m and leaf counts from \NLeavesMin{} to \NLeavesMax{}.

\subsection{Exact procedural wind}
We deliberately do not use physics simulation or a node-based deformer: the same closed-form function both moves the rendered vertices and produces the exported ground truth, so the two are identical by construction.

\paragraph{Branch sway} Every skeleton node $j$ receives a bend rotation
\begin{equation}
\Delta_j(t) = \mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf b, \theta_j(t)\big)\,\mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf w, \psi_j(t)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf w$ is the horizontal wind direction and $\mathbf b = \mathbf e_z \times \mathbf w$. Rotations are accumulated from the root by forward kinematics, $A_j = A_{p(j)}\Delta_j$ and $\mathbf p_j = \mathbf p_{p(j)} + A_{p(j)}(\mathbf p_j^0 - \mathbf p_{p(j)}^0)$, so displacement grows with height and branching order. The bend and cross-wind angles are
\begin{align}
\theta_j &= c_j\, g_b(U_e)\,[\alpha_s + \alpha_o \sin(2\pi f_{B}t + \phi_{B})], \\
\psi_j &= c_j\, g_b(U_e)\,\alpha_c \sin(2\pi r f_{B} t + \phi'_{B}),
\end{align}
with frequency $f_B$ and phase $\phi_B$ shared by all nodes of a branch $B$ (coherent sway), and a compliance $c_j \propto (r_0/r_j)\,\ell_j$ that favours thin, long segments.

\paragraph{Leaf flutter} Each leaf rotates rigidly about its petiole attachment,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
R_\ell = {}& \mathrm{Rot}(\mathbf m_\ell, g_l\beta_m)\,\mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf x_\ell, g_l\beta_t \sin(2\pi r_\ell f_\ell t + \phi'_\ell)\big) \\
& \mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf y_\ell, g_l\beta_p\sin(2\pi f_\ell t + \phi_\ell)\big),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with a per-leaf frequency $f_\ell\in[2.5, 9]$\,Hz, mean down-wind reconfiguration about $\mathbf m_\ell = \mathbf n_\ell \times \mathbf w$, and is carried by its node: $A_\ell = A_{n(\ell)}R_\ell$. The leaf's rigid transform from the canonical pose, $\mathbf x_t = A_\ell \mathbf x_0 + \mathbf t_\ell$, is exported per frame.

\paragraph{Amplitude laws and gusts} $g_b(U) = (U/U_r)^2$ follows the quadratic dependence of drag on speed for small elastic deflections; $g_l(U) = s\tanh\big((U/U_r)/s\big)$ lets flutter grow roughly linearly and saturate. Gusts modulate the speed, $U_e(t) = U\,\max\{0,\,1+\gamma\,n(t)\}$, with a smooth seeded noise $n(t)$. All angles vanish exactly at $U=0$ and rotations are evaluated element-wise, so calm sequences are the rest pose bit-for-bit. Phases depend only on the tree seed, so different wind levels of a tree differ only in amplitude. Over \NFrames{} frames, the median leaf displacement at \WindMax\,m/s ranges from \WindDispHiMin{} to \WindDispHiMax\,mm across trees, with median leaf rotations of \WindRotHiMin--\WindRotHiMax$^\circ$ (\cref{fig:wind}). The amplitudes are plausible defaults chosen so that leaves move by millimetres in light wind and by several centimetres at \WindMax\,m/s; they are configurable, and a calibration procedure against static-camera fan recordings is provided (\cref{sec:calib}). Because absolute amplitudes are not tied to a specific plant and fan, the resulting leaf motion is reported alongside $U$ (\cref{fig:wind}).

\subsection{D435i-like rendering and ground-truth passes}
The camera uses D435i-like pinhole intrinsics for the $848\times480$ colour stream (nominal values, reproduced exactly in Blender through focal length, principal point via lens shift and pixel aspect, and replaceable by the calibrated values of a specific device) and a hand-held orbit with seeded smooth jitter; the trajectory depends only on the tree seed, so it is bit-identical across wind levels. Time is explicit: frame $i$ is rendered at $t = i\,k/\text{fps}$ for both wind and camera.

RGB is rendered with EEVEE or Cycles and converted to 8-bit sRGB with the exact transfer function. Ground truth is rendered in a separate Cycles pass with one sample per pixel and a 0.01-pixel box filter, so every value is the first hit of the ray through the pixel centre and is never blended across edges. Shader outputs store per-pixel world position, normal, leaf identity, semantic class, rest position and next-frame position. Depth is computed as $z = (\mathbf P - \mathbf C)\cdot \mathbf o$ from the world position, so it is z-depth by construction. Exact optical flow is obtained by projecting the interpolated next-frame position, which also covers non-rigid wood deformation. Motion blur and rolling shutter can be rendered by sub-frame and row-band integration, but \emph{both are disabled in every experiment reported here}: all RGB frames are instantaneous global-shutter images, which is optimistic relative to real hand-held capture of moving foliage (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Sensor noise model}\label{sec:noise}
Clean data are always kept and a noisy copy is derived. Depth undergoes resampling to the sensor's effective depth resolution; spatially correlated noise with $\sigma(z) = a + b z^2$ or, alternatively, Gaussian disparity noise with sub-pixel quantisation; flying pixels that interpolate foreground and background depth at discontinuities; holes whose probability increases near edges, on structures thinner than a few pixels and at grazing angles; range limits; misregistration; and quantisation to millimetres. RGB receives an auto-exposure random walk, slight blur, shot and read noise and JPEG compression. Noise draws are keyed to the tree and frame, so they are paired across wind levels.

\subsection{Calibration tooling}\label{sec:calib}
All experiments in this paper use D435-like default noise parameters ($\sigma(z) = 0.5\,\text{mm} + 3.8\,\text{mm\,m}^{-2} z^2$, i.e.\ about 4\,mm at 1\,m) and default wind amplitudes. To adapt SynthTree to a specific device and fan, the generator includes calibration procedures. The noise parameters are fitted from recordings of a flat textured wall at six distances (plane-fit residuals give $a$, $b$ and the spatial correlation; invalid pixels give the random hole rate) and of leaves against a distant background (vegetation silhouettes give flying-pixel, edge-hole and thin-structure-hole rates by fixed-point matching of simulated statistics). On synthetic recordings with known parameters, the procedure recovers $a$, $b$ and the correlation length within 3\,\% and the flying-pixel and hole rates within 2\,\%. Wind amplitudes can be calibrated analogously by matching the median metric leaf speed (optical flow scaled by depth) of static-camera fan clips, iterating until all wind levels agree within 3\,\%; a self-test with a known amplitude factor of 1.6 recovers 1.57.

\subsection{Validation}
Every sequence is validated automatically on the exported products (\cref{tab:validation}); all \NSeqPassed{} of \NSeqValidated{} sequences pass. The decisive check for depth convention is geometric: back-projecting the depth of the floor yields points within \ValGroundUm\,$\mu$m of the plane $z=0$ even in the image corners, whereas interpreting the same values as ray distance would displace them by \ValRayMm\,mm.

\section{Benchmark protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Data and splits} Each tree is rendered at four canopy wind speeds, $U \in \{\WindLevels\}$\,m/s, where $U=0$ is the calm sequence, giving \NSeq{} sequences for \NTrees{} trees; each sequence is a single orbit of \NFrames{} frames at the sensor resolution ($848\times480$). Every eighth frame is held out for view-synthesis evaluation. All experiments use trees 1, 3, 5 and 7, one per leaf type (\cref{tab:trees}); in tables, low, medium and high denote $U = 1.5$, 3 and 5\,m/s. No generalisation experiments are reported. Motion blur and rolling shutter are disabled throughout.

\paragraph{Methods} We evaluate splatfacto (3DGS) and depth-nerfacto (NeRF with depth supervision) from Nerfstudio \citep{tancik2023nerfstudio}, trained with the ground-truth poses in metric world coordinates (no re-centring or re-scaling) and initialised from back-projected sensor depth of the same variant (clean or noisy). To test whether modelling motion helps, we additionally implement, on the same gsplat rasteriser, a static 3DGS and a deformable 3DGS that share initialisation, losses (0.8\,L1 + 0.2\,D-SSIM), densification, background and iteration budget (10{,}000); the deformable variant adds a time-conditioned MLP (8 layers, 256 units) that predicts per-Gaussian offsets of position, rotation and scale from positionally encoded canonical position and time \citep{yang2024deformable}, enabled after 2{,}000 static warm-up iterations. As a simple mitigation that requires no motion model, a third variant replaces the L1 term by a trimmed L1 that ignores the 20\,\% of pixels with the largest residual in each training view (after 1{,}000 iterations), so that moving leaves the static model cannot explain do not pull Gaussians into blurred compromises.

\paragraph{Geometry-first evaluation} Reconstructions are compared with the canonical leaf surface. Each reconstructed point is attributed to its nearest ground-truth surface (leaf, wood, pot/soil or floor); points farther than 3\,cm from every surface are floaters and are reported separately. For Gaussians, precision-side quantities (accuracy, precision, floaters, per-leaf centroids, leaf angles) use Gaussian centres, so each splat counts once, whereas coverage-side quantities (completeness, recall, recovered leaf area, leaf coverage) use samples spread over each Gaussian's footprint, because centres alone under-cover thin surfaces. We report the F-score at $\tau = 5$\,mm, a capped chamfer distance, the relative leaf-area error, the 1-D Wasserstein distance between leaf inclination distributions \citep{ross1981radiation}, and the median per-leaf centroid error. Needles are thinner than a pixel, so for conifers per-leaf quantities are computed per shoot (all needles of a twig). View-synthesis PSNR is reported on held-out views.

\paragraph{Rest-pose and posed evaluation} The canonical (rest) surface is the natural target when the goal is the plant's architecture, but under wind the true surface at any instant is displaced from it. Because SynthTree exports the rigid transform of every leaf at every frame, we also evaluate \emph{posed}: the leaf surface is posed with the transforms of a held-out frame and compared with the reconstruction at that frame's time (the same reconstruction for static methods, the deformed one for dynamic methods), averaged over four held-out frames.

\paragraph{Three-way decomposition} For a metric $E$ oriented so that larger is worse,
\begin{align}
E_{\text{method}} &= E(\text{calm}, \text{clean}), \\
E_{\text{sensor}} &= E(\text{calm}, \text{noisy}) - E(\text{calm}, \text{clean}), \\
E_{\text{wind}}(U) &= E(U, \text{noisy}) - E(\text{calm}, \text{noisy}), \\
E_{\text{int}}(U) &= \big[E(U,\text{noisy}) - E(U,\text{clean})\big] - E_{\text{sensor}}.
\end{align}
The interaction term measures how far sensor and wind errors are from additive. Values are averaged over seeds per configuration, and mean and standard deviation over trees are reported.

\paragraph{Calm and clean (occlusion/method error)} Even with exact poses and noise-free depth, splatfacto reaches only F@5mm = \SpBaseF{} and a chamfer distance of \SpBaseCD\,mm on leaves (\cref{tab:decomp}), and inclines leaves more steeply than they are; this is the intrinsic difficulty of thin, self-occluding foliage. Depth-supervised depth-nerfacto recovers markedly better calm geometry (F@5mm = \NfBaseF{}, chamfer distance \NfBaseCD\,mm) with almost no floaters.

\paragraph{Wind} Wind is the dominant degradation. For splatfacto at \WindMax\,m/s the leaf F-score drops by \SpWindHiF{}, the chamfer distance increases by \SpWindHiCD\,mm and the per-leaf (per-shoot for the conifer) centroid error by \SpWindHiCentroid\,mm (\cref{fig:decomp}); the seed-averaged F-score decreases monotonically with wind speed on all four trees, and per seed in \SpMonoCount{} of \SpMonoTotal{} tree--seed pairs. Depth-nerfacto loses a comparable amount (F-score $-$\NfWindHiF{}, chamfer distance $+$\NfWindHiCD\,mm), so its better calm geometry does not protect it against leaf motion; for the conifer its single-seed curve is not monotonic, which more seeds must resolve. Broad-, small- and compound-leaved trees degrade most; the needle tree, whose shoots move less (\cref{fig:wind}), degrades least. Per tree, the median leaf displacement at \WindMax\,m/s, the absolute F-score drop and the relative drop of splatfacto are: \MotionDropList. The absolute drop follows the amount of leaf motion (Spearman $\rho = \MotionDropRho$; $\rho = \MotionDropRhoRel$ for the relative drop), but with $n = \MotionDropN$ trees this is descriptive, not a significance claim; leaf type and canopy density vary together with motion across our trees.

\paragraph{Sensor} For splatfacto the sensor term is negative in F-score (\SpSensorF{}) and chamfer distance (\SpSensorCD\,mm), i.e.\ noisy input appears to \emph{help}. The components explain why: with noisy RGB-D input, splatfacto converges to \SpGaussRatio$\times$ fewer Gaussians near the tree (\SpGaussNoisy{} vs.\ \SpGaussClean{}), which are larger, so recall rises from \SpRecClean{} to \SpRecNoisy{} while precision is unchanged (\SpPrecClean{} vs.\ \SpPrecNoisy{}) and the floater fraction grows from \SpFloatClean{} to \SpFloatNoisy{}. Sensor noise thus trades geometric precision for coverage, and aggregate scores such as the F-score or a capped chamfer distance can mistake this for an improvement; we therefore recommend reporting precision and recall separately. For depth-nerfacto, which is supervised by the depth itself, the sensor term is positive (\NfSensorF{} in F-score) as expected. Because the noise parameters are D435-like defaults rather than values calibrated to one device, the magnitude of the sensor term is specific to this noise model; the qualitative precision--coverage trade-off is the transferable finding.

\paragraph{Interaction} For splatfacto at \WindMax\,m/s the interaction is $+1.4 \pm 1.5$\,mm in chamfer distance and $-0.8 \pm 2.1$\,mm in centroid error, against wind terms of $+\SpWindHiCD$ and $+\SpWindHiCentroid$\,mm (\cref{tab:decomp}); for depth-nerfacto (two trees) it is $+2.2 \pm 3.1$\,mm in chamfer distance. The interaction is thus small relative to the wind term, but its tree-to-tree spread is as large as its mean: within the resolution of four trees we see no evidence of strong non-additivity, which is weaker than establishing that sensor and wind errors are additive.

\paragraph{Rest pose versus posed ground truth} For static 3DGS (\NDynTrees{} trees, noisy sensor), the calm-to-\WindMax\,m/s drop in F-score is \StWindDropRest{} against the rest pose but only \StWindDropPosed{} against the posed leaves (\cref{tab:dynamic,fig:dynamic}): at \WindMax\,m/s the reconstruction matches the moving canopy (F@5mm = \StPosedFHi{}) better than the rest pose (\StRestFHi{}). Posed F exceeds rest-pose F in \StPosedBeatsRest{} of \StPosedConds{} windy tree--wind conditions. A static reconstruction of a windy plant converges towards its mean, down-wind-deflected shape, so rest-pose evaluation over-states the wind error by \StWindDropOverPct\,\% here; the time-resolved per-leaf ground truth is needed to separate deflection from blur. This figure is specific to our static 3DGS on four trees with one seed and noisy input; whether other static methods behave alike was not tested.

\paragraph{Deformable baseline}\label{sec:dynamic} The deformable model does not recover the geometry lost to wind: at \WindMax\,m/s its posed F@5mm is \DyPosedFHi{} against \StPosedFHi{} for the static model, and held-out PSNR rises by only 0.1--0.3\,dB (\cref{tab:dynamic,fig:dynamic}). The learned deformation explains why. Its per-Gaussian offsets have a median of \DyOffMin--\DyOffMax\,mm with a 95th-percentile-to-median ratio of \DyOffRatio, and the offset is no larger in strong wind (\DyOffHi\,mm) than in calm air (\DyOffCalm\,mm): the field collapses to a nearly uniform shift instead of leaf-specific, wind-dependent motion. We used a temporal encoding with ten frequency bands, because the six bands of \citet{yang2024deformable} limit temporal variation to about 0.8\,Hz over a 20\,s orbit, below branch-sway (0.6--2\,Hz) and flutter (2.5--9\,Hz) frequencies; a first round with six bands (16 runs, superseded and not counted in \cref{tab:runs}) behaved identically. Three properties of the capture make the problem hard for any deformation field: each instant is observed from a single viewpoint, frames are 0.13\,s apart so flutter above 3.75\,Hz is aliased, and the motion consists of thousands of small, independent rigid parts. These results characterise one representative design trained for 8{,}000 deformation iterations; dedicated dynamic methods or longer schedules may fare better, and SynthTree's per-leaf ground truth is what makes such claims testable. Whether the collapse is caused by the low frame rate or by the motion model is tested directly in \cref{sec:stage1}.

\paragraph{Mitigation} The trimmed loss improves the posed F-score over plain static 3DGS in \RbWins{} of \RbConds{} tree--wind conditions (one-sided sign test $p = \RbSignP$, treating conditions as independent although they share four trees), raising precision and recall together, lowering the posed chamfer distance by \RbCDGainHi\,mm at \WindMax\,m/s and reducing floaters (\cref{tab:dynamic}); held-out PSNR is essentially unchanged. The gain, however, is largest in calm air ($+$\RbGainCalm{}) and smaller at \WindMax\,m/s ($+$\RbGainHi{}), and the calm-to-\WindMax\,m/s drop of the trimmed variant (\RbWindDrop{}) is as large as that of plain 3DGS (\StWindDropPosed{}). Down-weighting poorly explained pixels is thus a cheap and consistently beneficial robustness measure, but it reduces the method/sensor component rather than the wind component of the error: once leaves move, the residual is not confined to a minority of outlier pixels that can be discarded.

\subsection{Follow-up: sampling or modelling?}\label{sec:stage1}
The frames used above are 0.13\,s apart, and \SoAliasPctMin--\SoAliasPctMax\,\% of the energy of the ground-truth leaf-normal trajectories lies above their 3.75\,Hz Nyquist limit (closed-form wind model at \WindMax\,m/s, trees 1, 3, 5 and 7). The collapse of the deformable baseline could therefore be caused by temporal sampling rather than by the motion model. To separate the two, we re-rendered the four trees, calm and at \WindMax\,m/s, at the full 30\,fps (600 frames per orbit; identical trees, wind phases and camera path) and trained every model on all frames (30\,fps) and on every fourth frame (7.5\,fps, the rate used above), so that both rates share the renders and the held-out views.

\caption{Reconstruction model. Canonical Gaussians, initialised from back-projected sensor depth, are moved to the time $t$ of a training view by a motion module, the only component that differs between the methods compared; the moved Gaussians are rendered with the gsplat rasteriser from the known camera pose (no camera tracking is needed) and compared with the training view. Gradients update both the Gaussians and the motion parameters. The two oracles (dashed) use ground-truth leaf identities or leaf motion and serve as upper bounds. \paragraph{Per-part rigid motion} As a probe of motion structure, not as a new method (\cref{fig:model}), we add a deliberately simple per-part model in the spirit of superpoint and control-point Gaussians \citep{wan2024superpoint,huang2024scgs}. After the static warm-up, every Gaussian is assigned to the nearest of 2{,}048 k-means cells of the warm-up geometry and to one of 32 coarser branch-scale cells. Each cell $k$ follows its own rigid trajectory $x \mapsto R_k(t)\,(x-p_k)+p_k+v_k(t)$, whose rotation vector and translation are Fourier series in time up to 10\,Hz, unlocked coarse to fine, with an acceleration penalty that is ten times stronger for branch cells; a Gaussian moves with its branch cell composed with its leaf-scale cell. Because cells own independent parameters, they cannot be smoothed into one global shift the way a deformation network can. An \emph{oracle} variant replaces the k-means cells by the ground-truth leaf identities (one cell per leaf; Gaussians farther than 1\,cm from every leaf keep k-means cells). It uses information no real capture provides and serves only as an upper bound for the grouping. All models use the schedule above (10{,}000 iterations, 2{,}000 static warm-up) and noisy input. Static, per-cell and oracle models at \WindMax\,m/s are trained with \SoNSeedsHi{} seeds, whose posed F-scores differ by at most \SoSeedSdMax{} (standard deviation per tree); seeds are averaged per tree before averaging over trees, and all other conditions use one seed (\SoNRuns{} runs in total, \cref{tab:stage1}). These runs use new renders, a newer release of the gsplat rasteriser (1.5.3 instead of 1.4.0) and a newer software environment. On tree 1 the deformable baseline at 7.5\,fps reaches posed F@5mm of \SoReproNewCalm{} (calm) and \SoReproNewHi{} (\WindMax\,m/s) against \SoReproOldCalm{} and \SoReproOldHi{} in \cref{tab:dynamic}. A control isolates the rasteriser: static 3DGS at 7.5\,fps on tree 1 and the new renders reaches \SoChkFiveCalm{} (calm) and \SoChkFiveHi{} (\WindMax\,m/s) with gsplat 1.5.3 and \SoChkFourCalm{} and \SoChkFourHi{} with gsplat 1.4.0, against \SoChkPaperCalm{} and \SoChkPaperHi{} above. The rasteriser version thus explains only a small part of the offset, which stems mainly from the new renders and environment; absolute values are lower than above and only comparisons within \cref{tab:stage1} are drawn.

\paragraph{The collapse is a modelling failure} At 30\,fps the deformable model still learns a uniform shift: its offsets have a median of \SoDyOffMed\,mm and a 95th percentile of \SoDyOffPninetyfive\,mm (95th-percentile-to-median ratio \SoDyOffRatioMin{} on every tree), and its posed F-score at \WindMax\,m/s (\SoDyFHi) stays below that of static 3DGS (\SoStFHi). Four times denser sampling does not prevent the collapse. The ground-truth motion indicates why a smooth field fails: the leaf-normal trajectories need \SoRankNormalMin--\SoRankNormalMax{} singular components to hold 90\,\% of their energy, and a single rigid transform per frame explains only \SoEtaMin--\SoEtaMax{} of the leaf-centroid motion. Independently fluttering leaves therefore cannot be represented by one smooth or low-rank deformation, which is also what shared motion bases assume \citep{wang2024shape}.

\paragraph{Per-part motion removes the collapse but recovers little geometry} The per-cell model learns leaf-specific motion (offset median \SoClOffMed\,mm, 95th percentile \SoClOffPninetyfive\,mm; ratio \SoClOffRatioMin--\SoClOffRatioMax). Compared with static 3DGS it reduces the wind part of the posed chamfer distance from \SoStWindCD{} to \SoClWindCD\,mm ($-$\SoClWindCDRedPct\,\%) and raises held-out PSNR by \SoClPSNRGain\,dB, and lowers the posed chamfer distance on \SoClBeatsStCD{} of 4 trees, but its posed F-score at \WindMax\,m/s (\SoClFHi) is not higher than that of static 3DGS (\SoStFHi): it is clearly higher only on tree 1 and within the seed spread on trees 3 and 5. On the needle tree, where one cell spans many needles, it is markedly worse (\SoNeedleClFHi{} vs.\ \SoNeedleStFHi), and it introduces spurious motion in calm air (calm F \SoNeedleClFCalm{} vs.\ \SoNeedleStFCalm{} there), so part of the smaller wind term comes from a worse calm reconstruction rather than a better windy one. With oracle groups the posed F-score rises to \SoOrFHi{} and the wind part of the chamfer distance falls by \SoOrWindCDRedPct\,\%: even perfect per-leaf grouping leaves about three quarters of the wind-induced chamfer error.

\paragraph{Denser sampling does not help without blur} Trained on every fourth frame, the per-cell model reaches the same geometry (posed F \SoClFHiSlow, chamfer distance \SoClCDHiSlow\,mm) as with all frames (\SoClFHi, \SoClCDHi\,mm). Without motion blur the frame rate is thus not what limits these reconstructions: evaluated at frame times, an aliased trajectory fits the samples as well as the true one, and each instant is still observed from a single viewpoint. The camera turns by about 2$^\circ$ during one 9\,Hz flutter period, so the hand-held orbit is effectively monocular for leaf flutter \citep{gao2022monocular}.

\paragraph{A second synchronised camera does not help either} To test whether this single-viewpoint observation is the limit, we rendered a second camera that follows the same orbit 90$^\circ$ ahead, synchronised frame by frame, and trained on the views of both (test views and ground truth remain those of the first camera). The second viewpoint leaves the wind part of the posed chamfer distance unchanged for static 3DGS (\SoStWindCD{} vs.\ \SoStMvWindCD\,mm with one and two cameras) and for the per-cell model (\SoClWindCD{} vs.\ \SoClMvWindCD\,mm), and reduces it only slightly with oracle groups (\SoOrWindCD{} vs.\ \SoOrMvWindCD\,mm); the calm-to-\WindMax\,m/s drop in posed F-score is likewise unchanged (static \SoStWindF{} vs.\ \SoStMvWindF, per-cell \SoClWindF{} vs.\ \SoClMvWindF, oracle \SoOrWindF{} vs.\ \SoOrMvWindF). Absolute scores are slightly lower with two cameras, also in calm air (static calm F \SoStFCalm{} vs.\ \SoStMvFCalm), which we attribute to the fixed iteration budget being shared by twice as many views; because the second camera follows the same orbit, it adds no viewing directions of a static scene over the whole sequence, only simultaneous ones. Within this design, viewpoint coverage per instant is therefore not the limit either. \Cref{fig:recon_images,fig:recon_geometry} show the effect qualitatively for tree 1: the rendered views remain plausible in wind, while much of the true leaf surface at that instant is not covered by any reconstruction.

\paragraph{Learned motion falls far short of the true motion} What remains is the estimation of leaf motion itself. The ground-truth leaf centroids move by \SoGtMotionRmsMin--\SoGtMotionRmsMax\,mm (RMS about their mean) at \WindMax\,m/s, whereas even with oracle groups the learned offsets have a median of \SoOrOffMed\,mm and a 95th percentile of \SoOrOffPninetyfive\,mm. The motion models thus move Gaussians in the right places but by far too little, with one or two cameras and at either frame rate, which points to the recovery of large, fast, independent leaf motion from photometric supervision, rather than the representation of that motion, as the remaining bottleneck.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
All conclusions in this paper are drawn from synthetic data. The sensor-noise parameters and wind amplitudes are plausible defaults rather than values calibrated to a particular camera, plant and fan, so absolute error magnitudes may differ on real captures; the controlled comparisons (calm versus windy, clean versus noisy, method versus method), which are what the paired design measures, do not depend on these exact values. Confirming the error-versus-wind trends on real captures, using the calibration tooling of \cref{sec:calib}, is the most important next step. The generator also trades biomechanical fidelity for exactness: branch bending is piecewise rigid along skeleton segments, compound leaves move as single rigid units, leaves may interpenetrate, gusts are spatially uniform, and wind amplitudes and noise parameters are defaults (\cref{sec:calib}). Motion blur and rolling shutter, which a real hand-held capture of moving leaves would add, were disabled in all experiments; the reported wind errors are therefore likely lower bounds for real RGB capture. When enabled, they affect RGB only, and ground truth refers to the frame-centre time, as for a global-shutter depth sensor. Rendered pixels are deterministic per machine but not across GPUs, whereas geometry, poses and ground truth are bit-identical. Our conclusions concern the implementations tested: splatfacto and depth-nerfacto from Nerfstudio and our gsplat-based static, trimmed-loss and deformable 3DGS. Surface-oriented splatting such as 2DGS \citep{huang20242dgs}, other NeRF variants and RGB-D SLAM were not evaluated, and the benchmark covers four trees with one or two seeds, so per-tree effects are descriptive. Our deformable and per-part models are single, deliberately simple designs. The per-part model is a probe, not a contribution: closely related superpoint, control-point and motion-basis Gaussians exist \citep{wan2024superpoint,huang2024scgs,wang2024shape}, and dedicated dynamic methods \citep{wu20244dgs,luiten2024dynamic} were not evaluated. The 30\,fps follow-up (\cref{sec:stage1}) uses new renders and a newer software environment, so its absolute values are not directly comparable to the main benchmark; a control shows that the rasteriser version accounts for only a small part of the offset. Its calm runs and its 7.5\,fps runs use one seed. The multi-view probe used two cameras 90$^\circ$ apart on one orbit and a fixed iteration budget; rigs with more or wider-spaced cameras, longer schedules, motion blur, and motion priors or supervision that help recover large leaf motion (for example from optical flow, which SynthTree exports) are natural next steps, and the exported per-leaf transforms allow their motion to be evaluated directly.

\section{Conclusion}
SynthTree provides paired calm/windy RGB-D sequences of plants with exact per-leaf ground truth and a protocol that separates occlusion, sensor and wind error. In simulation, under default sensor and wind models and across four leaf types and the methods tested, wind, not sensor noise, is the dominant source of geometric error for neural foliage reconstruction; image-space metrics conceal most of it, rest-pose evaluation over-states it for our static baseline, and neither the deformation-field baseline nor the robust photometric loss we tested recovers it. The collapse of the deformation field persists at 30\,fps and is a failure of the motion model rather than of temporal sampling; per-part rigid motion removes it, but even with ground-truth leaf grouping it recovers only about a quarter of the wind-induced chamfer error. Neither denser temporal sampling nor a second synchronised camera reduces the wind error, and the learned leaf motion stays far below the true motion, so recovering large, fast leaf motion from images is the remaining bottleneck. Confirming these trends on real captures is the next step.
