\title{Leaf motion limits 3D reconstruction of plants in wind: a synthetic benchmark and a study of per-leaf motion models}

\author[1]{Bibek Gyawali\corref{cor1}}
\ead{TODO-institutional-email@example.org}

\begin{abstract}
3D Gaussian splatting and neural radiance fields are now used to reconstruct plants, but they assume that nothing in the scene moves. Leaves move whenever there is wind. We built SynthTree, a generator of synthetic RGB-D videos of potted saplings in which every tree is filmed twice along the same camera path: once in still air and once in wind. Leaves are real geometry and are moved by a simple closed-form wind model, so the true position of every leaf is known in every frame; automatic checks on all \NSeqValidated{} sequences confirm that this ground truth matches the images to within \ValLeafFlowPx\,px. In \NRunsTotal{} training runs on four trees, wind at \WindMax\,m/s lowers the leaf F-score of 3D Gaussian splatting by \SpWindHiF{} and of a depth-supervised NeRF by \NfWindHiF{}, while image quality (PSNR) changes by only \RelChangePSNR\,\%: the images look right although the leaves are in the wrong place. Simulated image and depth noise matters much less than wind. A standard deformable Gaussian model does not help, because its learned motion collapses into one small shift of the whole tree. We then asked why. The collapse remains at 30 frames per second, so the frame rate is not the cause. A per-leaf motion model removes the collapse and reduces the wind part of the chamfer error by \SoClWindCDRedPct\,\%, but even when points are grouped into leaves using the true leaf identities, about three quarters of that error remain, and a second synchronised camera does not help. The learned leaf motion stays far smaller than the true motion. When the same model is given the true leaf motion, the wind error disappears (F-score \SoGtFHi{} in wind against \SoGtFCalm{} in calm air), so estimating fast leaf motion from images, not representing it, is the main open problem. All results come from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
3D models of plants are used to measure leaf area, leaf angles and canopy shape without harming the plant \citep{paulus2019crops3d}. Neural radiance fields \citep{mildenhall2020nerf,muller2022instant} and 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) \citep{kerbl2023gaussian} can build such models from an ordinary video, and they are quickly being adopted for plants. They all assume a still scene. Real leaves rarely keep still: in a light breeze they flutter several times per second and whole branches sway \citep{delangre2008wind}, while filming a full circle around a sapling takes tens of seconds.

When the 3D model of a real plant is poor, it is hard to say why. Thin leaves that hide each other are difficult for any method, image and depth data are noisy, and moving leaves break the still-scene assumption. On real data these causes cannot be told apart, because nobody knows where every leaf was at every moment. Existing datasets show still scenes \citep{jensen2014dtu,knapitsch2017tanks,sturm2012tum} or plant point clouds without leaf motion \citep{schunck2021pheno4d}.

We therefore built a synthetic benchmark in which every factor is controlled and every leaf is tracked exactly, and used it to study both how much wind hurts reconstruction and what limits methods that try to model the motion. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{SynthTree}, an open, fully scripted generator of procedural saplings, wind and RGB-D video with optional image and depth noise. It produces \emph{paired} sequences: the same tree and the same camera path, once calm and once windy.
\item \textbf{Exact per-leaf ground truth in wind}: leaf identity images and the position and orientation of every leaf in every frame, checked to sub-pixel accuracy on all sequences.
\item \textbf{An evaluation method} that compares reconstructions with the true leaf surface at each moment and splits the error into a method part, a noise part and a wind part.
\item \textbf{A benchmark} showing that wind is the main source of error, that image metrics hide most of it, and that comparing with the still (rest) pose overstates it.
\item \textbf{A study of motion models}: a per-leaf motion model removes the collapse of a standard deformable model but recovers little geometry, neither a higher frame rate nor a second camera helps, and the learned motion is far too small. Given the true leaf motion, the same model reconstructs windy leaves as well as calm ones, so estimating the motion, not representing it, is the bottleneck.
\end{enumerate}
Code and data are available (\cref{sec:availability}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Plant reconstruction} Laser scanning, multi-view stereo and RGB-D cameras are established tools for measuring plant shape \citep{paulus2019crops3d}. Pheno4D \citep{schunck2021pheno4d} provides point clouds of growing plants with organ labels, and organ tracking between scans has been studied over hours and days \citep{li2013growing}. At those time scales, wind during one scan is treated as noise. The way plants move in wind, from slow branch sway to fast leaf flutter, is well described in biomechanics \citep{delangre2008wind}, but its effect on reconstruction accuracy has not been measured. Real datasets are recorded in still air and cannot provide the true shape of moving leaves.

\paragraph{Reconstructing still and moving scenes} NeRF \citep{mildenhall2020nerf}, its faster variants \citep{muller2022instant}, 3DGS \citep{kerbl2023gaussian} and the surface-based 2DGS \citep{huang20242dgs} reconstruct still scenes; we use the implementations in Nerfstudio \citep{tancik2023nerfstudio}. Dynamic versions learn how the scene deforms over time \citep{pumarola2021dnerf,luiten2024dynamic,wu20244dgs,yang2024deformable}. Some split the scene into parts that move rigidly, such as superpoints \citep{wan2024superpoint} or sparse control points \citep{huang2024scgs}, or share a small set of motion bases between all points \citep{wang2024shape}; others give each Gaussian its own trajectory \citep{lin2024gaussianflow}. With one moving camera, how well motion can be recovered depends on how far the camera moves compared with the scene \citep{gao2022monocular}. Robust variants ignore content that does not fit, either with per-image appearance codes \citep{martinbrualla2021nerfw} or by down-weighting pixels with large errors \citep{sabour2023robustnerf}; our trimmed loss follows the second idea. None of these methods has been tested on fluttering leaves against ground truth.

\paragraph{Synthetic data} Rendering pipelines such as BlenderProc \citep{denninger2023blenderproc2} and ICL-NUIM \citep{handa2014iclnuim} give exact ground truth for rigid indoor scenes, and procedural plant models have a long history \citep{prusinkiewicz1990abop,weber1995trees,runions2007space}. As far as we know, no benchmark provides paired still and windy plants with per-leaf motion ground truth.

\subsection{Trees with leaf identities}
Each tree is a skeleton of branches grown with random bends, an upward tendency and regular branching patterns \citep{weber1995trees}. The wood is a tube mesh around the skeleton. Leaves are real triangle meshes with species-specific outlines, cupping and droop; compound leaves have leaflets on a stalk, and needles are thin prisms. Every leaf has a number, an attachment point, a parent branch, a rest pose, an area and a centre (\cref{tab:trees}). Trees are \HeightMin{} to \HeightMax\,m tall and have \NLeavesMin{} to \NLeavesMax{} leaves.

\subsection{Wind}
We do not run a physics simulation. Instead, one closed-form function moves the rendered leaves and also produces the exported ground truth, so the two always agree.

\paragraph{Branch sway} Every skeleton node $j$ bends by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_j(t) = \mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf b, \theta_j(t)\big)\,\mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf w, \psi_j(t)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf w$ is the wind direction and $\mathbf b = \mathbf e_z \times \mathbf w$. Bends add up from the root to the tips, $A_j = A_{p(j)}\Delta_j$ and $\mathbf p_j = \mathbf p_{p(j)} + A_{p(j)}(\mathbf p_j^0 - \mathbf p_{p(j)}^0)$, so outer branches move most. The bend angles are
\begin{align}
\theta_j &= c_j\, g_b(U_e)\,[\alpha_s + \alpha_o \sin(2\pi f_{B}t + \phi_{B})], \\
\psi_j &= c_j\, g_b(U_e)\,\alpha_c \sin(2\pi r f_{B} t + \phi'_{B}),
\end{align}
with one frequency $f_B$ and phase $\phi_B$ per branch $B$, and a flexibility $c_j \propto (r_0/r_j)\,\ell_j$ that makes thin, long segments bend more.

\paragraph{Leaf flutter} Each leaf rotates as a rigid body about its stalk,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
R_\ell = {}& \mathrm{Rot}(\mathbf m_\ell, g_l\beta_m)\,\mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf x_\ell, g_l\beta_t \sin(2\pi r_\ell f_\ell t + \phi'_\ell)\big) \\
& \mathrm{Rot}\big(\mathbf y_\ell, g_l\beta_p\sin(2\pi f_\ell t + \phi_\ell)\big),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with its own frequency $f_\ell$ between 2.5 and 9\,Hz and a mean tilt away from the wind about $\mathbf m_\ell = \mathbf n_\ell \times \mathbf w$. The leaf also follows its branch, $A_\ell = A_{n(\ell)}R_\ell$, and its transform from the rest pose, $\mathbf x_t = A_\ell \mathbf x_0 + \mathbf t_\ell$, is saved for every frame.

\paragraph{Wind strength and gusts} Branch bending grows with the square of the wind speed, $g_b(U) = (U/U_r)^2$, like drag on a flexible body; leaf flutter grows roughly linearly and then levels off, $g_l(U) = s\tanh\big((U/U_r)/s\big)$. Gusts vary the speed smoothly over time, $U_e(t) = U\,\max\{0,\,1+\gamma\,n(t)\}$. All angles are exactly zero at $U=0$, so calm videos show the tree exactly in its rest pose. The random phases depend only on the tree, so the wind levels of one tree differ only in strength. At \WindMax\,m/s the median leaf moves by \WindDispHiMin{} to \WindDispHiMax\,mm and turns by \WindRotHiMin--\WindRotHiMax$^\circ$, depending on the tree (\cref{fig:wind}). These amplitudes are plausible defaults, not measurements of a particular plant, so we report leaf motion alongside wind speed.

\subsection{Rendering and ground truth}
A pinhole camera with an $848\times480$ image circles the tree with small random hand-held shaking. The camera path depends only on the tree, so it is identical for all wind levels. Colour images are rendered with Blender's EEVEE or Cycles renderer. Ground truth is rendered in a separate pass with one sample per pixel, so values are never blended across leaf edges. For each pixel we save the 3D position, normal, leaf number, class (leaf, wood, pot, floor) and the position in the next frame, from which we get exact depth and optical flow. Motion blur and rolling shutter can be rendered but are \emph{switched off in all experiments}: every image is a sharp snapshot, which is kinder to the methods than a real handheld recording would be (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Image and depth noise}\label{sec:noise}
Every sequence is saved clean and with simulated noise. The noisy depth is lower in resolution and has errors that grow with distance, $\sigma(z) = a + b z^2$ (about 4\,mm at 1\,m by default); false ``flying'' points between leaves and background at depth edges; holes that are more likely near edges, on thin structures and on surfaces seen at a steep angle; and rounding to millimetres. The noisy colour image has small exposure changes, slight blur, pixel noise and JPEG compression. The noise is identical for all wind levels of a tree, so calm and windy runs differ only in the wind. The noise and wind parameters are configurable.

\subsection{Checks}
Every sequence is checked automatically (\cref{tab:validation}); all \NSeqPassed{} of \NSeqValidated{} pass. For example, floor points recovered from the depth lie within \ValGroundUm\,$\mu$m of the true floor even in the image corners; using the wrong depth convention would move them by \ValRayMm\,mm.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Data} Each tree is rendered at four wind speeds, $U \in \{\WindLevels\}$\,m/s, where 0 is calm, giving \NSeq{} sequences for \NTrees{} trees. In the main benchmark each sequence has \NFrames{} frames, one every 0.13\,s (every fourth frame of a 30\,fps video). Every eighth frame is held out for testing. We use trees 1, 3, 5 and 7, one per leaf type; low, medium and high wind mean 1.5, 3 and 5\,m/s.

\paragraph{Methods} We test splatfacto (3DGS) and depth-nerfacto (a NeRF that also uses depth) from Nerfstudio \citep{tancik2023nerfstudio}, given the true camera poses and started from the depth points of the same (clean or noisy) data. To test whether modelling motion helps, we also implemented our own static 3DGS and a deformable 3DGS on the same gsplat renderer, with the same start, loss (0.8\,L1 + 0.2\,D-SSIM), point management and 10{,}000 iterations. The deformable model adds a small neural network (8 layers, 256 units) that moves each Gaussian depending on its position and the time \citep{yang2024deformable}, switched on after 2{,}000 iterations without motion. A third variant, the trimmed loss, ignores the 20\,\% of pixels with the largest error in each view, so that leaves the model cannot explain do not pull the geometry into a blur.

\paragraph{Measuring geometry} We compare reconstructions with the true leaf surface. Each reconstructed point is assigned to the nearest true surface (leaf, wood, pot or floor); points more than 3\,cm from everything are counted as floaters. Accuracy-type measures use the Gaussian centres, so each Gaussian counts once. Coverage-type measures use points spread over each Gaussian's footprint, because the centres alone would make thin leaves look badly covered. We report the leaf F-score at 5\,mm, a capped chamfer distance, the leaf-area error, the difference between leaf-angle distributions \citep{ross1981radiation} and the median error of leaf centres. Needles are thinner than a pixel, so for needle trees we treat each twig as one unit. Image quality is measured with PSNR on held-out views.

\paragraph{Rest pose or true pose} One can compare a reconstruction with the tree's rest pose (its shape in still air) or with the true position of every leaf at the moment of a test frame, which SynthTree also provides. We report both, calling the second \emph{posed}; for dynamic methods the reconstruction is moved to that moment first. Posed scores are averaged over four held-out frames.

\paragraph{Splitting the error} For any error measure $E$ (larger is worse) we define
\begin{align}
E_{\text{method}} &= E(\text{calm}, \text{clean}), \\
E_{\text{noise}} &= E(\text{calm}, \text{noisy}) - E(\text{calm}, \text{clean}), \\
E_{\text{wind}}(U) &= E(U, \text{noisy}) - E(\text{calm}, \text{noisy}), \\
E_{\text{int}}(U) &= \big[E(U,\text{noisy}) - E(U,\text{clean})\big] - E_{\text{noise}}.
\end{align}
The last term shows whether noise and wind simply add up. We average over seeds and report the mean and spread over trees.

\paragraph{Calm air} Even with exact camera poses and clean data, splatfacto reaches only F@5mm = \SpBaseF{} and a chamfer distance of \SpBaseCD\,mm on leaves (\cref{tab:decomp}), and it tilts leaves more steeply than they are. Thin leaves that hide each other are simply hard. Depth-nerfacto, which also learns from depth, does clearly better in calm air (F@5mm = \NfBaseF{}, chamfer distance \NfBaseCD\,mm) and has almost no floaters.

\paragraph{Wind} Wind causes the largest damage. For splatfacto at \WindMax\,m/s the leaf F-score drops by \SpWindHiF{}, the chamfer distance rises by \SpWindHiCD\,mm and the error of leaf centres by \SpWindHiCentroid\,mm (\cref{fig:decomp}). The F-score falls steadily with wind speed on all four trees, and for \SpMonoCount{} of \SpMonoTotal{} tree--seed pairs. Depth-nerfacto loses about as much (F-score $-$\NfWindHiF{}, chamfer distance $+$\NfWindHiCD\,mm), so its better calm result does not protect it from moving leaves. Trees with broad, small and compound leaves suffer most; the needle tree, whose twigs move least (\cref{fig:wind}), suffers least. Per tree, the median leaf motion at \WindMax\,m/s, the absolute F-score drop and the relative drop are: \MotionDropList. Trees whose leaves move more lose more (Spearman $\rho = \MotionDropRho$; $\rho = \MotionDropRhoRel$ for the relative drop), but with $n = \MotionDropN$ trees this is a description, not a statistical test.

\paragraph{Noise} For splatfacto the noise term is negative (F-score \SpSensorF{}, chamfer distance \SpSensorCD\,mm): noisy data seem to \emph{help}. The reason is that with noisy data splatfacto ends with \SpGaussRatio$\times$ fewer but larger Gaussians near the tree (\SpGaussNoisy{} vs.\ \SpGaussClean{}). They cover more of the leaves (recall \SpRecClean{} $\to$ \SpRecNoisy{}) without being more precise (precision \SpPrecClean{} vs.\ \SpPrecNoisy{}), and floaters increase (\SpFloatClean{} $\to$ \SpFloatNoisy{}). Noise therefore trades precision for coverage, and a single combined score can mistake this for an improvement, so precision and recall should be reported separately. For depth-nerfacto, which learns directly from the depth, noise hurts as expected (F-score \NfSensorF{}). The size of the noise effect depends on our default noise settings; the trade-off between precision and coverage is the general lesson.

\paragraph{Do noise and wind add up?} At \WindMax\,m/s the interaction term for splatfacto is $+1.4 \pm 1.5$\,mm in chamfer distance and $-0.8 \pm 2.1$\,mm in centre error, small compared with the wind terms of $+\SpWindHiCD$ and $+\SpWindHiCentroid$\,mm (\cref{tab:decomp}); for depth-nerfacto it is $+2.2 \pm 3.1$\,mm. With four trees we see no sign that noise and wind interact strongly, but we cannot prove that they simply add up.

\paragraph{Rest pose versus true pose} For static 3DGS (\NDynTrees{} trees, noisy data), the F-score drop from calm to \WindMax\,m/s is \StWindDropRest{} when compared with the rest pose but only \StWindDropPosed{} when compared with the true leaf positions (\cref{tab:dynamic,fig:dynamic}). At \WindMax\,m/s the reconstruction matches the moving leaves (F@5mm = \StPosedFHi{}) better than the rest pose (\StRestFHi{}), in \StPosedBeatsRest{} of \StPosedConds{} windy cases. A static model of a windy plant converges to its average, wind-bent shape, so comparing with the rest pose overstates the wind error by \StWindDropOverPct\,\%. This holds for our static 3DGS on four trees with one seed.

\paragraph{Deformable model}\label{sec:dynamic} The deformable model does not recover the geometry lost to wind: at \WindMax\,m/s its posed F@5mm is \DyPosedFHi{}, against \StPosedFHi{} for the static model, and PSNR rises by only 0.1--0.3\,dB (\cref{tab:dynamic,fig:dynamic}). Its learned motion shows why. The Gaussians move by a median of \DyOffMin--\DyOffMax\,mm, almost all by the same amount (95th percentile to median ratio \DyOffRatio), and no more in strong wind (\DyOffHi\,mm) than in calm air (\DyOffCalm\,mm). The deformation has collapsed into a nearly uniform shift of the whole tree instead of moving individual leaves. We gave the network ten time frequencies instead of the usual six \citep{yang2024deformable}, because six only allow changes slower than about 0.8\,Hz over a 20\,s video; a first round with six behaved the same. Three things make the task hard: each moment is seen from only one viewpoint, frames are 0.13\,s apart so flutter above 3.75\,Hz is under-sampled, and thousands of small leaves move independently. \Cref{sec:stage1} tests which of these matters.

\paragraph{Trimmed loss} Ignoring the worst-fitting pixels improves the posed F-score over plain static 3DGS in \RbWins{} of \RbConds{} tree--wind cases (one-sided sign test $p = \RbSignP$), raises precision and recall, lowers the chamfer distance by \RbCDGainHi\,mm at \WindMax\,m/s and removes floaters (\cref{tab:dynamic}). But the gain is largest in calm air ($+$\RbGainCalm{}) and smaller in strong wind ($+$\RbGainHi{}), and the drop from calm to \WindMax\,m/s (\RbWindDrop{}) is as large as without it (\StWindDropPosed{}). It is a cheap, useful fix for the method error, not for the wind error: once leaves move, the bad pixels are no longer a small minority that can be ignored.

\section{Can motion models recover the leaves?}\label{sec:stage1}
The frames of the main benchmark are 0.13\,s apart, and \SoAliasPctMin--\SoAliasPctMax\,\% of the leaf motion happens faster than these frames can capture (above 3.75\,Hz). The collapse of the deformable model could therefore be caused by the low frame rate rather than by the model. To separate the two, we rendered trees 1, 3, 5 and 7 again, calm and at \WindMax\,m/s, at the full 30\,fps (600 frames, same trees, wind and camera path), and trained every model on all frames (30\,fps) and on every fourth frame (7.5\,fps, as in the benchmark).

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{model_architecture.png}
\caption{The reconstruction model. Gaussians are moved to the time of a training image by a motion module (the only part that differs between methods), rendered from the known camera pose and compared with the image. The two oracles (dashed) use ground truth.}
\label{fig:model}
\end{figure}

\subsection{A per-leaf motion model}
To test whether the collapse comes from the model's structure, we built a simple per-leaf motion model, similar in spirit to superpoint and control-point Gaussians \citep{wan2024superpoint,huang2024scgs} (\cref{fig:model}). After the warm-up, the Gaussians are grouped by position into 2{,}048 small cells, about the size of a leaf, and 32 large cells, about the size of a branch. Each cell moves as a rigid body, $x \mapsto R_k(t)\,(x-p_k)+p_k+v_k(t)$, along its own path in time, described by a Fourier series up to 10\,Hz whose higher frequencies are added gradually. A Gaussian follows its branch cell and its leaf cell. Branch cells are penalised ten times more for fast changes. Because every cell has its own parameters, the cells cannot merge into one global shift.

We also test two \emph{oracles}, which use information a real recording does not have and therefore only show what would be possible. The first groups the Gaussians by the true leaf identities (one cell per leaf). The second also gives each leaf its true motion, so that no leaf motion has to be learned.

All models use the same schedule as before (10{,}000 iterations, 2{,}000 without motion) and noisy data. At \WindMax\,m/s the static, per-leaf and oracle models were trained with \SoNSeedsHi{} seeds, whose F-scores differ by at most \SoSeedSdMax{}; other conditions use one seed (\SoNRuns{} runs in total, \cref{tab:stage1}). These runs use new renders, a newer version of the gsplat renderer (1.5.3 instead of 1.4.0) and newer software, which lowers all scores a little: on tree 1 the deformable model at 7.5\,fps scores \SoReproNewCalm{} (calm) and \SoReproNewHi{} (windy) against \SoReproOldCalm{} and \SoReproOldHi{} in \cref{tab:dynamic}. Re-running static 3DGS with the old renderer version on the new data gives \SoChkFourCalm{} and \SoChkFourHi{} (new version: \SoChkFiveCalm{} and \SoChkFiveHi{}; benchmark: \SoChkPaperCalm{} and \SoChkPaperHi{}), so the renderer version explains only a small part of the difference. We therefore only compare runs within \cref{tab:stage1}.

\subsection{The collapse is a modelling problem}
At 30\,fps the deformable model still learns a uniform shift: its offsets have a median of \SoDyOffMed\,mm and a 95th percentile of \SoDyOffPninetyfive\,mm (ratio \SoDyOffRatioMin{} on every tree), and its F-score at \WindMax\,m/s (\SoDyFHi) stays below that of static 3DGS (\SoStFHi). Four times more frames do not prevent the collapse. The true leaf motion explains why one smooth deformation cannot describe it: \SoRankNormalMin--\SoRankNormalMax{} independent components are needed to describe 90\,\% of the leaf orientations, and a single rigid motion of the whole tree explains only a fraction of \SoEtaMin--\SoEtaMax{} of the leaf-centre motion. Independently fluttering leaves do not fit a smooth or low-dimensional motion model, which is also what shared motion bases assume \citep{wang2024shape}.

\subsection{Per-leaf motion removes the collapse but recovers little}
The per-leaf model learns different motion for different leaves (offset median \SoClOffMed\,mm, 95th percentile \SoClOffPninetyfive\,mm; ratio \SoClOffRatioMin--\SoClOffRatioMax). Compared with static 3DGS it reduces the wind part of the chamfer distance from \SoStWindCD{} to \SoClWindCD\,mm ($-$\SoClWindCDRedPct\,\%), lowers the chamfer distance on \SoClBeatsStCD{} of 4 trees and raises PSNR by \SoClPSNRGain\,dB. Its F-score at \WindMax\,m/s (\SoClFHi), however, is not higher than that of static 3DGS (\SoStFHi): it is clearly higher only on tree 1 and within the seed spread on trees 3 and 5. On the needle tree, where one cell contains many needles, it is much worse (\SoNeedleClFHi{} vs.\ \SoNeedleStFHi), and it adds small false motion in calm air (calm F \SoNeedleClFCalm{} vs.\ \SoNeedleStFCalm{} there), so part of its smaller wind term comes from a worse calm result. With the true leaf grouping the F-score rises to \SoOrFHi{} and the wind part of the chamfer distance falls by \SoOrWindCDRedPct\,\%: even perfect grouping leaves about three quarters of the wind error.

\Cref{fig:conditions_clean,fig:conditions_noisy} show the per-leaf model on tree 1 in every condition of the benchmark. The reconstructed images are hard to tell apart from the real ones at every wind speed, but in 3D the share of reconstructed points within 5\,mm of a true leaf falls from \PrecCleanCalm\,\% in calm air to \PrecCleanHigh\,\% at \WindMax\,m/s with clean data (\PrecNoisyCalm\,\% to \PrecNoisyHigh\,\% with noisy data), and the share of the true leaf surface it covers from \CovCleanCalm\,\% to \CovCleanHigh\,\% (\CovNoisyCalm\,\% to \CovNoisyHigh\,\%).

\subsection{A higher frame rate does not help}
Trained on every fourth frame, the per-leaf model reaches the same geometry (F \SoClFHiSlow, chamfer distance \SoClCDHiSlow\,mm) as with all frames (\SoClFHi, \SoClCDHi\,mm). Without motion blur, the frame rate is not the limit: at the frame times, a slower trajectory fits the images as well as the true one, and each moment is still seen from only one viewpoint. The camera turns by only about 2$^\circ$ during one flutter cycle at 9\,Hz, so for leaf flutter the orbit is almost a single fixed view \citep{gao2022monocular}.

\subsection{A second camera does not help either}
To test whether the single viewpoint is the limit, we added a second camera that follows the same circle 90$^\circ$ ahead, synchronised frame by frame, and trained on the images of both (testing still uses the first camera). The wind part of the chamfer distance stays the same for static 3DGS (\SoStWindCD{} vs.\ \SoStMvWindCD\,mm with one and two cameras) and the per-leaf model (\SoClWindCD{} vs.\ \SoClMvWindCD\,mm), and falls only slightly with the true leaf grouping (\SoOrWindCD{} vs.\ \SoOrMvWindCD\,mm); the drop in F-score is also unchanged (static \SoStWindF{} vs.\ \SoStMvWindF, per-leaf \SoClWindF{} vs.\ \SoClMvWindF, oracle \SoOrWindF{} vs.\ \SoOrMvWindF). All scores are a little lower with two cameras, also in calm air (static calm F \SoStFCalm{} vs.\ \SoStMvFCalm), probably because the same number of iterations is shared by twice as many images. In this setup, seeing each moment from a second direction is not the limit. \Cref{fig:recon_images,fig:recon_geometry} compare the methods on tree 1.

\subsection{The learned motion is far too small}
What remains is estimating the leaf motion itself. The true leaf centres move by \SoGtMotionRmsMin--\SoGtMotionRmsMax\,mm at \WindMax\,m/s, but even with the true leaf grouping the learned motion has a median of only \SoOrOffMed\,mm and a 95th percentile of \SoOrOffPninetyfive\,mm. The models move the right Gaussians, but by far too little, with one or two cameras and at both frame rates.

\subsection{With the true motion, the wind error disappears}
The second oracle settles the question. It is the same model with the same leaf grouping, but each leaf follows its true motion instead of a learned one. At \WindMax\,m/s it reaches a posed F-score of \SoGtFHi{} (calm: \SoGtFCalm), a chamfer distance of \SoGtCDHi\,mm and a leaf-centre error of \SoGtCentroidHi\,mm (calm: \SoGtCentroidCalm\,mm), against \SoStFHi{}, \SoStCDHi\,mm and \SoStCentroidHi\,mm for static 3DGS (\cref{tab:stage1}). The wind part of the chamfer distance drops from \SoStWindCD\,mm to \SoGtWindCD\,mm, and PSNR rises to \SoGtPSNRHi\,dB. On \SoGtBeatsCalm{} of 4 trees the windy reconstruction is even better than the calm one, probably because moving leaves show more of their surface to the camera over time; only the needle tree, where single needles are hard to group, gains little (F \SoGtNeedleFHi). Two cameras give the same result (F \SoGtMvFHi, wind part \SoGtMvWindCD\,mm). In \cref{fig:recon_images,fig:recon_geometry} it is the only model whose windy leaves stay sharp and on the true surface. The representation is therefore good enough: once the motion is known, Gaussians reconstruct leaves in wind as well as in still air. Estimating large, fast, independent leaf motion from images is the remaining problem.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
All results come from simulation. The noise and wind settings are plausible defaults, not values measured for a particular plant and fan, so the absolute errors on real recordings may differ. The comparisons that the paired design is built for (calm against windy, clean against noisy, one method against another) do not depend on these exact values. Checking the trends on real recordings is the most important next step.

The generator trades physical realism for exact ground truth: branches bend in rigid segments, compound leaves move as one piece, leaves can pass through each other and gusts are the same everywhere. Motion blur and rolling shutter, which a real handheld recording of moving leaves would add, were switched off, so the wind errors reported here are probably lower than they would be in practice.

Our conclusions apply to the methods we tested: splatfacto and depth-nerfacto from Nerfstudio and our static, trimmed-loss, deformable and per-leaf 3DGS. We did not test 2DGS \citep{huang20242dgs}, other NeRF variants or dedicated dynamic methods \citep{wu20244dgs,luiten2024dynamic}, and with four trees and one to three seeds the per-tree results are descriptive. The per-leaf model is a simple test of motion structure, not a new method; closely related models exist \citep{wan2024superpoint,huang2024scgs,wang2024shape}. The motion-model study uses new renders and newer software, so its absolute numbers are not directly comparable with the benchmark; its calm and 7.5\,fps runs use one seed. The second-camera test used two cameras 90$^\circ$ apart on one circle and a fixed number of iterations. More or more widely spaced cameras, longer training, motion blur, and extra guidance for large leaf motion, for example from the optical flow that SynthTree saves, are natural next steps, and the saved per-leaf motion allows them to be evaluated exactly.

\section{Conclusion}
SynthTree provides paired calm and windy RGB-D videos of plants with exact per-leaf ground truth and a way to split reconstruction error into method, noise and wind. In simulation, across four leaf types, wind is the main source of error for neural plant reconstruction; image metrics hide most of it, comparing with the rest pose overstates it, and neither a deformable model nor a robust loss recovers it. The deformable model fails because its motion collapses into a uniform shift, and this happens at 30\,fps as well, so the model, not the frame rate, is to blame. Per-leaf motion removes the collapse, but even with the true leaf grouping it recovers only about a quarter of the wind error, and neither more frames nor a second camera help. The learned leaf motion stays far smaller than the true motion, and when the true motion is given, the wind error disappears. Recovering fast leaf motion from images is therefore the key problem to solve next, and the trends should be checked on real recordings.
